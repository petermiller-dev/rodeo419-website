03Process

Six steps from first conversation to a site you're proud of

You'll always know what's happening, what I need from you, and what comes next. Most of the work is on my side, but a few steps need you, and those are the ones that make the site sound like your business.

  1. 01

    First conversation

    We talk about your business, what's working, and what isn't. No pitch.

  2. 02

    Discovery

    I learn how your business earns trust and what customers ask before they buy.

  3. 03

    Design and copy

    I write the pages and design the site around them, with a photo plan to match.

  4. 04

    Review

    You click through the preview, mark what's wrong, and we refine it together.

  5. 05

    Launch

    We connect your domain, set up search basics, and make the site public.

  6. 06

    Ongoing care

    Updates, hosting oversight, and a regular check-in, if you want them.

01

First conversation

We start with a conversation, by phone or in person around Fort Worth. I'll ask what you do, who your best customers are, how they usually find you, and what you'd like to change. If you have a website, we'll look at it together.

By the end, you'll know whether I'm the right fit and what I'd suggest. If I'm not the right fit, I'll tell you.

Where you come in

Tell me about your business, your customers, and what's frustrating you about your website or Google listing. Have your current site handy if you have one.

02

Discovery

This is where the messaging work happens. I'll ask the questions your customers ask, look at how competitors present themselves, review your Google Business Profile, and figure out which pages you need. You'll get a short summary of what I heard and a proposed site map before any design starts, so we agree on the plan.

Where you come in

An hour or two of conversation, plus any photos, reviews, brochures, or notes you already have. Introduce me to anyone else who should weigh in.

03

Design and copy

I write the copy and design the pages together, because each one shapes the other. You'll get a working preview, a real website you can open on your phone, not a static mockup. Every photo slot is planned and labeled so we know exactly what to shoot.

Where you come in

Review drafts when I send them. If we're planning photography, help schedule the shoot or take the photos on the list.

04

Review

You know your business better than I do, so this step matters. I'll give you a short checklist of open decisions, things like the details only you can confirm, and we'll go through the preview together. Then I make the changes and we look again until it's right.

Where you come in

Read every page carefully. Check facts, prices, hours, and anything with your name on it. Tell me what doesn't sound like you.

05

Launch

When you approve the site, I connect your domain, turn on search indexing, submit the sitemap, and make sure your Google Business Profile points to the new site. I test it on phones and computers after it's live, not just before.

Where you come in

Approve the final version. Help me get access to your domain and Google Business Profile if I don't already have it.

06

Ongoing care

After launch, you can take the site over yourself or I can keep it up for you. Care plans cover updates, hosting oversight, keeping your Google profile in step with your website, and a periodic check-in on what to improve next.

Where you come in

Send me changes as they come up: new hours, new services, new work worth showing.

Common question

How this compares to WordPress, Wix, and Lovable

Owners often ask why I don't build on a platform they've heard of. Each of these can produce a good website. Here's how they differ from a custom-coded site, with sources so you can check the details yourself.

What about WordPress?

WordPress can be fast. With a caching plugin, it can save pages as static files and serve those to visitors. But how fast a WordPress site is depends on the individual build: the hosting, the caching setup, the theme, the plugins, and the images and scripts each page loads. In HTTP Archive's October 2026 mobile data, 49% of the WordPress sites it measured passed all three Core Web Vitals. That figure describes the sites measured, not what any one WordPress site will do. Sources: WordPress caching documentation (opens in a new tab), HTTP Archive CMS report (opens in a new tab).

What about Wix?

Wix takes care of hosting and updates for you, and in HTTP Archive's October 2026 mobile data, 81% of the Wix sites it measured passed all three Core Web Vitals. Again, that describes the sites measured, not a prediction for yours. The bigger difference is where the site can live. A standard Wix site has to run on Wix's servers. Wix's Studio Two editor offers a beta export you can run on your own Node.js host, but the exported site still loads fonts and media from Wix and calls Wix services for business features, and some features can't be exported yet. Sources: Wix: exporting or embedding your site (opens in a new tab), Wix: host an exported site (opens in a new tab), Wix: about exporting (opens in a new tab), HTTP Archive CMS report (opens in a new tab).

What about Lovable?

Lovable builds web applications from written prompts. Since May 2026, new Lovable projects use TanStack Start, a full-stack React framework that can render pages on a server, run server-side functions, and connect to a database. That's a sensible foundation for an app with accounts and data. A local business marketing site usually needs less: a handful of pages that look the same to every visitor, built ahead of time as plain HTML and CSS, with a small script for the few features that need one. Lovable can prerender pages like that too, but they remain part of a React application that's built and deployed as one project. I build the lighter kind of site directly. Source: Lovable: Building apps using TanStack Start (opens in a new tab).

Lab scores and field data

The Core Web Vitals figures above are field data: measurements from real visits to those sites. A Lighthouse score is a lab test: one simulated page load under fixed settings. They answer different questions, so I keep them separate. When I share a Lighthouse score for my own work, I'll name the page, the date, and the test settings with it.
