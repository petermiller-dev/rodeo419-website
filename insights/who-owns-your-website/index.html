Who really owns your website?

A website is really four or five separate things, and each one can be held by a different person. Here's how to find out who controls each piece, and how to bring them back under your name.

The short version

  • Your domain should be registered to your business, with an email address you control.
  • Paying a designer doesn't automatically make you the copyright owner of their work. A signed, written agreement settles it.
  • Some website builders only run on their own servers. Know what you can take with you before you build.
  • Make sure you are the primary owner of your Google Business Profile.

Ask a business owner who owns their website and most will say, "I do. I paid for it." Usually that's true. Sometimes it isn't, and owners tend to find out at a bad moment: when a designer stops answering email, when the employee who set everything up moves on, or when they want to change hosts and learn they can't.

Part of the confusion is that "the website" isn't one thing. It's a domain name, a place where the site is hosted, the design and code, the words and photos, and, for a local business, a Google Business Profile. Each piece can be held by a different person. Here's what to check for each one.

The domain name

Your domain is your address on the web: yourbusiness.com. It's registered through a company called a registrar, and the person or business listed as the registrant is the one who holds it.

ICANN, the nonprofit that coordinates domain names, lists what registrants are entitled to and responsible for. One responsibility is simple and easy to miss: "You must provide accurate information for publication in directories such as WHOIS, and promptly update this to reflect any changes." ICANN also states that "it is your right to transfer your domain name registrations between registrars."

The common problem is innocent. A designer registers the domain in their own account to save the owner a step. Years later, the renewal notices go to the designer's inbox, and moving the domain means tracking that person down. Timing matters too. ICANN notes that a registrar may deny a transfer within 60 days of a domain's first registration, and that a change of registrant can trigger a 60-day lock on transfers. It's easier to fix ownership before you need to move than in the middle of a move.

What to check: log in to your registrar yourself. The registrant should be your business, and the account email should be one you'll still have in five years: a business address, not the personal inbox of someone who might leave.

The hosting

Hosting is where your site's files live and get served to visitors. Who controls it, and whether you can move it, depends on how the site was built.

Some website builders are the site and the host in one product. Wix, for example, says plainly in its help center that "in order for your site to work properly, it needs to be hosted and operated on Wix's servers." It also says the content you build belongs to you. That's a reasonable trade for many businesses: hosting and software updates are handled for you. The catch is that leaving usually means rebuilding the site somewhere else, so it's worth knowing before you start.

A site built from standard files can usually be moved to another host, as long as you can get a complete copy.

What to check: whose account pays for hosting, whether you can download a full copy of the site, and whether another developer could run it without the person who built it.

The design and the code

This is the piece that surprises people most. Under U.S. copyright law, "the copyright in a work initially belongs to the author(s) who created that work." Paying for something doesn't change that by itself. The Copyright Office also notes that owning a copy of a work does not give you ownership of the copyright in it.

There is an exception called "work made for hire." Work created by your own employees as part of their job is one form of it. Work by an outside contractor is different: according to the Copyright Office, a commissioned work counts as made for hire only if it falls into one of nine specific categories and both parties sign a written agreement saying so. "If a work fails to satisfy any of these requirements, it is not a work made for hire." Copyright can also be transferred, but a transfer "generally must be made in writing and signed by the owner of the rights conveyed."

In practice: if you hired an outside designer and never signed anything about ownership, you may not hold the copyright in the design and code they wrote. Some parts of most sites were never the designer's to give away, either. Licensed fonts, themes, and stock photos come with their own license terms.

This is general information about U.S. copyright, not legal advice. If you're writing or reviewing a contract, a business attorney can tell you how it applies to you.

What to check: whoever you hire, ask for a written agreement that says who owns the finished design, code, and content, and what you receive if you part ways.

Your words and photos

The text you wrote and the photos you took are yours to begin with, and they're often the most valuable part of the site. They're also the easiest to lose track of. Keep the originals: full-size photos, logo files, and the text of your pages, somewhere that doesn't depend on the website.

How easily content comes back out varies. Some systems store pages in a database that's only readable through their software. Others keep each page as a plain file you can open on any computer.

Your Google Business Profile

For many local businesses, the Google listing brings in more calls than the website does. Google separates two roles. In Google's words, "As the owner, you have full control over the profile. You can add or remove users, manage profile info, and even delete the profile. A Business Profile can have multiple owners but only one primary owner." Managers have nearly the same access, except "they can't add or remove users or remove the profile."

Google also suggests that each person use their own Google account, so nobody needs to share a password. The arrangement that holds up best: you are the primary owner, and anyone helping you, whether an employee or an agency, is added as a manager.

A short ownership checklist

  1. Log in to your domain registrar yourself, and confirm the registrant and the account email are yours.
  2. Know where the site is hosted, whose account pays for it, and how you would get a full copy.
  3. Have a signed, written agreement that says who owns the design, code, and content.
  4. Keep your own copies of photos, logos, and page text.
  5. Make sure you're the primary owner of your Google Business Profile, and give helpers manager access.
  6. Write down where each login lives, and keep that list somewhere safe.

None of this is a reason to distrust the people you work with. It's the same reason you keep the title to your truck in your own name: things change, and the paperwork should still point to you.
