Why website speed matters for small businesses

Speed isn't about winning a score. It's about whether a customer on a phone can read your page, trust it, and call you before they give up. Here's what the research shows, and where it stops.

The short version

  • Most of your customers carry a smartphone. Pew Research puts it at 91% of U.S. adults.
  • Google measures three things, called Core Web Vitals: how fast the main content appears, how quickly the page responds, and whether it holds still.
  • Google uses Core Web Vitals in ranking, but says good scores don't guarantee top results. Helpful content still matters most.
  • In one multi-industry study, a 0.1-second faster mobile site went along with more conversions and lower bounce rates.

Picture a customer standing in a parking lot, phone in hand, comparing three businesses that do what you do. One page shows them what they need almost at once. Another is still loading its photos. The third jumps around just as they go to tap the phone number.

They don't think about speed. They just pick the one that worked. That's the real reason speed matters for a small business: it decides whether people can use your site in the moments they need it.

Your customers are on phones

Pew Research Center's October 2026 figures put smartphone ownership at 91% of U.S. adults, up from 35% in its first survey on the question in 2011. For a local business, phones are often where the first impression happens: in a truck, in a waiting room, on a spotty connection. A page that's quick on office Wi-Fi can feel very different on a phone signal.

What "fast" actually means

Google publishes three measures, called Core Web Vitals, along with the targets it considers good:

  • Loading. The main content should appear within 2.5 seconds of the page starting to load. Google calls this Largest Contentful Paint.
  • Responsiveness. When someone taps or clicks, the page should respond within 200 milliseconds. This is Interaction to Next Paint.
  • Stability. Things on the page shouldn't jump around while it loads. The measure is Cumulative Layout Shift, and the target is 0.1 or less.

Google recommends judging these at "the 75th percentile of page loads, segmented across mobile and desktop devices." In plain terms, a page passes when most real visits meet the targets, not just the fastest ones.

Why holding still matters as much as speed

Layout shift gets less attention than loading time, but customers feel it. Google's web.dev guide explains that unexpected shifts can cause people to "lose their place while reading if the text moves suddenly," or make them "click the wrong link or button." On a local business site, that's someone tapping Directions when they meant Call.

The usual causes are ordinary: images and videos without set dimensions, fonts that load at a different size, and content that's added after the page appears. Each one is fixable when the site is built.

What speed does, and doesn't do, for search

Google is clear that "Core Web Vitals are used by our ranking systems." It is equally clear about the limits. Good results in speed reports don't guarantee "that your pages will rank at the top of Google Search results," and "trying to get a perfect score just for SEO reasons may not be the best use of your time."

That's a fair summary. Speed supports good content; it doesn't replace it. A fast page that doesn't answer a customer's question won't outrank a helpful one for long.

What speed does for business

The most cited research on this is Deloitte's 2020 study "Milliseconds Make Millions." Over four weeks, Deloitte analyzed mobile site data from retail, travel, luxury, and lead-generation brands in Europe and the U.S. With a 0.1-second improvement in mobile site speed, it observed that retail conversions rose 8.4% and travel conversions rose 10.1%. For lead-generation sites, the kind closest to a local service business, the bounce rate on information pages improved by 8.3%.

Read those numbers with care. The study looked at large brands, it describes a correlation rather than a promise, and it's from 2020. Your results won't match it. The direction is consistent, though: when pages respond faster, more people keep going.

What usually slows a small business site down

It's rarely one dramatic problem. More often it's a handful of ordinary ones: photos uploaded at full camera size, several outside tools each loading their own code (chat widgets, trackers, embedded maps and videos), and themes or page builders that load features the site doesn't use. Each choice can make sense on its own. Together they add weight that a customer on a phone has to wait for.

How to check your own site

  • Use your phone, on cellular, not Wi-Fi. Open your homepage and count how long it takes until you can read it and tap your phone number.
  • Look at real-visitor data if you have it. If your site is in Google Search Console, its Core Web Vitals report groups your pages by how real visitors experienced them.
  • Don't chase a perfect score. Google itself says it may not be the best use of your time. Fix what visitors notice first: slow first screens, jumpy layouts, and buttons that are hard to reach.
